CCTV-as-a-Service — A Failed Project, an Unvalidated Assumption, and the Lesson

Failed Project Lessons Learned Regulatory Assumption Sunk-Cost Discipline Ownership

Not every case study is a success story, and this one isn’t. I led the development of a managed CCTV-as-a-Service offering for the SMB market at Ooredoo, took it through several months of product, commercial, and operational design, and arrived at commercialization readiness only to discover that the regulatory approval I had assumed would come through would not. The product was shelved. The lesson is the part worth keeping, and it has shaped how I approach every regulated category since.

Context
One of Ooredoo’s strategic priorities was creating new value-added services and recurring revenue streams beyond core telecom connectivity. I identified what I believed was a strong opportunity in the SMB market: a managed CCTV-as-a-Service offering that bundled security cameras, installation, cloud-stored footage, maintenance, and support into a recurring monthly subscription rather than a large upfront capital purchase. The customer problem was real. SMBs in Qatar were buying surveillance systems from fragmented vendors with little operational support, and Ooredoo had the assets to integrate the experience end-to-end — nationwide connectivity infrastructure, field installation capability, billing systems, and existing SMB customer relationships.
The Approval Path I Assumed
I proposed the concept internally, received approval to proceed, and led the product development effort. Over several months, we built the commercial and operational model: pricing, packaging, partner integrations, technical architecture, installation workflows, and service operations. By the time we reached commercialization readiness, the product was substantively complete. The final step before launch was obtaining approval from Qatar’s Ministry of Interior (MOI), which regulates surveillance and security products. I believed approval would come through because we were partnering with licensed vendors to import, install, and maintain the surveillance technology rather than doing it ourselves.
1

What We Built — A Complete Managed-Service Offering

The product was real. Over several months we built the commercial model (subscription pricing, packaging, contract terms), the partner integration (licensed surveillance vendors for hardware and installation), the technical architecture (cloud-stored footage with retention policies and access controls), the operational workflows (installation, maintenance, support escalation), and the field execution model that tied into Ooredoo’s existing SMB account management. The development investment was substantial: pricing, packaging, partner agreements, technical architecture, installation workflows, service operations, and the GTM model were all designed and ready to ship.

2

Where I Got It Wrong — The Assumption

The mistake was specific and identifiable. I assumed that because we were only selling, while partnering with licensed vendors for the underlying surveillance technology, MOI approval would automatically follow. The reasoning felt logical inside the project: the vendors were licensed, the hardware was approved, and we were the commercial wrapper, not the regulated entity.

It was wrong. The Ministry of Interior viewed the managed-service model itself as sensitive, not just the underlying hardware. Their concerns became procedural and political rather than technical, and the approval timeline became highly uncertain. At that point the real challenge was no longer product development. The real challenge was recognizing whether we had built a commercially viable business around an unvalidated regulatory assumption.

3

The Decision to Shelve — Disagreeing, Then Committing

I continued advocating for the product. I believed sustained engagement with the regulator and additional architectural concessions could still unlock approval. My manager disagreed. He concluded that the regulatory uncertainty had fundamentally changed the business case and that continuing to invest leadership attention and organizational energy into the initiative was no longer justified relative to the other opportunities on the table.

After multiple discussions, he made the decision to shelve the product. In retrospect, his judgment was the right one. The right discipline at that point was not push harder on a stalled regulator. It was accept that the business case had changed and reallocate the leadership bandwidth. Watching a senior leader make that call cleanly, without sunk-cost reasoning, was itself a lesson worth carrying forward.
